Feedback needs a next step

A second attempt changes what students can do with feedback. It also changes what an instructor needs to design.

Assessment and feedback · October 18, 2026

A comment is not yet a learning opportunity. Students need a way to interpret it, decide what to change and try again.

In one Ethical Leadership presentation assignment, students researched a Sustainable Development Goal, presented to a small team, received peer feedback and presented a second time. The feedback had a job to do in the sequence of work. It was meant to describe observable behaviour, give specific evidence and point towards an action the presenter could take.

That makes the design question more demanding than “How much feedback should I give?” What will students do with it? When will they practise the change? Which requirements will the next attempt need to demonstrate? Who will make that assessment?

Peer feedback and grading also have different purposes. A listener can help a presenter hear where a message becomes unclear. The instructor still needs to judge whether the work meets the published criteria. Combining those roles without explaining them can leave everyone uncertain about what the comments mean.

The second attempt does not prove that learning has improved. It gives us another piece of work to examine and a more useful question to ask: what changed, and what does that change let us see?

The presentation feedback worksheet offers a way to plan that sequence. The Specifications Grading project puts it in the wider context of expectations, student choice and revision. Both are adaptations to think with, not a prescription for every course.
