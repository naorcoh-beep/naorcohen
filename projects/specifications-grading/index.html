The classroom account

Why I tried it

Grading can ask instructors to defend small differences in points while leaving students unsure what the work needs to demonstrate. I wanted to explore a different conversation: what counts as satisfactory work, which learning opportunities a student chooses, and what happens when an initial attempt falls short.

I used specifications grading in a Senior Leadership class focused on ethical leadership. Students were asked to connect ideas about leadership with judgement and action, not simply recall a set of concepts. Assessment needed to make the quality of that work visible while giving students room to choose how far to extend it.

The course offered another reason to reconsider assessment. If leadership involves shaping the conditions in which people act, the expectations and choices built into the course could themselves become something to examine. Clear criteria, nested bundles and a route to revision were intended to make those conditions explicit before students began the work.

Standards, choice and another attempt

The documented design used nested grade bundles. The common core combined participation with reading quizzes. A presentation and a final paper added further requirements. Required assignments were judged satisfactory or unsatisfactory against stated specifications, rather than awarded partial marks. The reading quizzes retained explicit percentage thresholds.

Choice was intended to be informed rather than hidden: students could see the work associated with a bundle before deciding what to undertake. Choosing a bundle did not itself earn the grade; its requirements still had to be met. Nor was the grade meant to stand for a student’s worth or potential.

Flexibility also needed rules. One course-policy version allocated two tokens that could be used for a resubmission, a submission up to 48 hours late, or an absence from a non-simulation class activity. Another iteration used a different revision allowance. These are distinct versions, not a single policy to combine. Anyone adapting the approach needs one consistent explanation of revisions, deadlines and exceptions.

A concrete example: feedback before a second presentation

In the Sustainable Development Goal assignment, students researched a goal and presented it to a small team. Peers offered developmental feedback, and each student presented again. The task was assessed individually even though parts of the activity happened in teams.

Useful feedback was part of the design: describe behaviour, give specific evidence and suggest a forward-looking action. The second presentation gave students an opportunity to decide what to change and demonstrate the work again. The instructor and teaching assistants remained responsible for assessment against the published specifications.

The leadership-philosophy and infographic tasks offer further examples of making expectations explicit. They should not be treated as interchangeable versions of the final paper or assumed to belong to a particular bundle. The accompanying worksheets therefore use selected principles to create new adaptation tools, rather than reproduce an entire course policy.

What remains difficult

Explicit criteria do not automatically produce shared understanding. Students need time to interpret the standards, see how the bundles work and understand what they can do after an unsatisfactory assessment. A binary judgement can feel particularly consequential when the route to revision is unclear.

I would also keep asking whether the more demanding bundles offer a meaningful extension of learning, whether students can navigate the choices, and whether the revision process is manageable for students and instructors. These are questions for course design, not details to leave until a dispute arises.

How to read this account

This account describes a teaching design and the questions it raises. Reflections on students’ responses can help identify what to investigate; they cannot, by themselves, establish that the approach improves learning or works better than another grading system.

For a first adaptation, begin with one task. Use the design worksheet to make its expectations and revision process clear, or try the presentation feedback sequence. The bundle explorer shows the core architecture of one course design without resolving policy differences on your behalf.
