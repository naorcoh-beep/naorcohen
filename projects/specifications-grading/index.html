The classroom account and revision policies

Why I tried it

I used specifications grading in a Senior Leadership class focused on ethical leadership. I wanted students to know what satisfactory work required and to choose how far to extend their work.

The design used nested grade bundles. Participation and reading quizzes formed the common core; a presentation and a final paper added requirements. Assignments were satisfactory or unsatisfactory against published specifications, while quizzes retained percentage thresholds. Choosing a bundle did not itself earn a grade.

Feedback before a second presentation

For the Sustainable Development Goal assignment, students researched a goal, presented to a small team, received peer feedback and presented again. Assessment was individual. Peers described observable behaviour and suggested a next step; the instructor and teaching assistants assessed the work against the specifications.

Revision rules need to be explicit

One policy version allocated two tokens for resubmission, work up to 48 hours late, or an absence from a non-simulation activity. Another iteration used a different revision allowance. These are separate versions: an adaptation needs one consistent policy, not a combination of both.

Students still need help interpreting standards, choosing a bundle and responding to an unsatisfactory result. This account documents a design, not evidence that it improves learning. The leadership-philosophy and infographic tasks are further examples of explicit expectations; they are not interchangeable final papers or automatically part of a particular bundle.
